Scottsville, Virginia

Scottsville at the Crossroads

Who gets to stay when a small town becomes desirable — told through the town's own history, its own numbers, and the county's own plan.

The Argument

Growth and Gentrification Are Not the Same Argument

A vocal minority of new arrivals says Scottsville "needs growth and development." Almost nobody asks: growth for whom, on whose terms?

The pro-growth case is real, and worth taking seriously. A whole housing region needs more supply somewhere, or prices rise everywhere — that's a mainstream, defensible argument. But it's an argument for building where the infrastructure and tax base already exist. As the numbers below show, that was never supposed to be Scottsville.

Growth

New housing and jobs the people already here can actually access. Tax revenue the town itself captures and controls. A larger community built around the one that exists, not instead of it.

Gentrification

New housing priced for people who aren't here yet. Tax revenue that flows to the county, not the town. A replacement community — Crozet's population didn't grow around its 2000 residents, it grew past them.

The test isn't whether the numbers go up. It's whether the people already here are still standing where they started when they do.

Albemarle County's own Comprehensive Plan

5%

of the county's land is Designated Growth Area — the Charlottesville urban ring, Crozet, Pantops, Village of Rivanna. This is where the plan directs new density.

95%

is Rural Area, where the county's stated policy is to discourage new development. Scottsville sits inside this 95%. It has never been a Designated Growth Area, unlike Crozet.

A live exception is being written in: Albemarle's newest plan (AC44, Oct. 2025) includes contested language letting Rural Area land get swapped into Growth Area status. Scottsville's own district supervisor said the mechanism "is not quite ready" but expects it to move forward.

The Argument, continued

A Real Government, With No Vote Where It Matters Most

Scottsville is the one place in the county with its own elected mayor and town council. Crozet has neither.

Inside town limits
Scottsville's mayor and six-member council set the town's own zoning, budget, and policing — real authority Crozet, with no government of its own, doesn't have.
Outside town limits — which is where it counts
West Village, the Tenaska plant, the Growth Area line itself: all decided entirely by Albemarle's Board of Supervisors. Scottsville's town government has no formal vote there at all.
The board seat is the same for everyone
The county's six-member Board of Supervisors gives Scottsville exactly one seat. Crozet, with no town government at all, gets the identical one seat.

Having a real government turns out to buy Scottsville nothing extra at the one table where the decisions that actually shape the town get made.

The Argument, continued

Two Kinds of Newcomer

It was never really about how long someone's lived here. It's about what they risked to arrive.

The Pioneer

Comes for affordability, not amenity. Lives in the same worn housing stock and flood risk as everyone already here. Invests sweat equity, not capital. Often ends up displaced too, once their own presence closes the gap that drew them.

The Buyer

Comes for a finished product, move-in ready, nothing left to absorb. The HOA is the point — insulation from the unpredictability everyone else shares. Consumes the town's character as a backdrop, not a relationship.

The pioneer can become a contributor, because arriving required risk and proximity to what already existed. The buyer structurally can't — the whole product they purchased was engineered to remove that friction.

History

A River Town, Contested Since Its Founding

Scottsville has never had the luxury of being settled. It's been fought over, flooded out, and rebuilt for nearly three centuries.

1744
Founded as Scott's Landing, when Albemarle County's courthouse was sited here — Edward Scott's own house served as the temporary courthouse.
1865
Union cavalry under Gen. Sheridan burned Scottsville in March, near the war's end. The same year, Union Baptist Church was organized on Hardware Road for Black worship — founded by a community that had almost certainly been enslaved in or near Scottsville itself, choosing to stay and build as free people.
1870–
Twenty-one major floods since. The worst: Hurricane Camille, August 1969 — the James crested at 30.5 feet, water reached the second story on Main Street. The 1985 Election Day flood put twelve feet through Bruce's Drugstore, the same store, twice in one generation.
1944–2004
A factory anchored the local economy for sixty years, then closed — taking jobs and tax revenue with it.
Today
543 residents inside town limits — 6.7% Black. Esmont, immediately adjacent and part of the same functional community, is 46.4% Black. The town-limits count hides how Black the wider community actually is.

Archival collections documenting Central Virginia history record "the slave market around Scottsville" directly — the town was an active point of the slave trade, not a bystander to it. Virginia's enslaved population reached roughly 40% of the state's total by the late 1700s. The Black families in and around Scottsville today are, in the overwhelming majority of cases, the direct descendants of people enslaved in this region — a lineage that runs deeper here than almost anyone currently in town government, or any newcomer drawn by the river views.

Reality Check

What "Revitalization" Actually Delivers

West Village is under construction now, on Scottsville's own doorstep.

What it promises
New housing addressing population decline, a larger local tax base, alignment with county growth targets.
What it's delivered
A construction workforce that commutes in and goes home elsewhere — no hiring pipeline into the town.
Where the money goes
Property tax revenue on the new homes flows to Albemarle County, not Scottsville — the same arrangement that's sent tax dollars to the county for over fifty years.

Tenaska: Promises Made, Promises Kept?

The original gas plant employs a staff of 28. A proposed second plant — nearly double the capacity — projects just 29 permanent jobs after 1,000+ temporary construction jobs. A third of the current staff live outside the county. Fluvanna's own Planning Commission put it plainly: "The community reports that they have not seen the benefits that were originally promised when Supervisors approved the first plant over 20 years ago." Tenaska is projected to become Fluvanna's largest taxpayer — Fluvanna's, not Scottsville's, despite the plant sitting on Scottsville's doorstep.

The Air Monitor Is in Charlottesville

Tenaska's safety claim cites "the closest regional monitor." That monitor is the DEQ station at Albemarle High School — in Charlottesville, over 20 miles from the plant, installed in 2008 to track county-wide ozone attainment. A DEQ specialist explained why that matters: "Where we place a monitor has a lot to do with the pollutant we want to measure." It isn't sited to capture what people near the plant actually breathe.

"Affordable Housing" Doesn't Mean Housing for Scottsville

Scottsville School Apartments — 34 units, constantly cited as proof the town does its part — is run by the Albemarle County Office of Housing and Piedmont Housing Alliance, headquartered in Charlottesville. Preferences go to homelessness, anyone who lives or works in Albemarle County, veterans, and domestic violence survivors. There is no Scottsville-resident preference at all. The most recent round filled by lottery. And because Scottsville straddles both Albemarle and Fluvanna counties, a poor family on the Fluvanna side of town doesn't even qualify for a building sitting inside their own town limits.

The Ledger

Who Actually Built This Town

Gave, without being asked

All-volunteer fire department since 1936. First-due EMS reaching into Fluvanna, Buckingham & Nelson Counties. Church networks. Neighbor-to-neighbor mutual aid after every flood. No payroll ever left town. No tax abatement was ever requested.

Took, while promising to give

A power plant taxed in Fluvanna, not Scottsville. A 1970s handshake deal trading levee protection for the town's own tax base — still in force. New development whose revenue flows to Albemarle. Sixty years of factory jobs, gone in one closure.

The Research

Fragile First: Who Actually Gets Displaced

Displacement doesn't hit a community evenly. It hits the parts of it with the least cushion first.

Less accumulated wealth to absorb a shock
Generations of exclusion from homeownership and lending mean Black families here typically have far less financial cushion to weather a rent increase or tax reassessment.
Networks that took generations to build
A church founded in 1865, a family on the same land for six generations — these aren't easily rebuilt elsewhere. One household priced out frays the network for everyone still there.
Nowhere comparable to land
Displaced low-income Black residents are typically pushed not into a similar neighborhood, but a poorer one — further from jobs, transit, and the community they came from.

What the Research Says

Displacement isn't just moving. "Exclusionary displacement" describes residents who aren't formally evicted but simply can't afford to stay — rising costs do the work eviction notices used to. A 2025 study found older Black residents report significantly higher fear of displacement even when they remain in place — researchers call this fear itself a health stressor, with roots in structural racism including historical redlining.

The toll on mental health is measurable. Residents in gentrifying tracts report more psychological distress than those in stable neighborhoods, concentrated among Black and Latino residents. Displaced residents show more emergency visits and hospitalizations for mental health than residents who stayed. Older Black residents report social isolation from the displacement of family and friends around them, even when never displaced themselves.

Housing instability compounds family strain. Sociologist Matthew Desmond's decade of eviction research found eviction is a cause of poverty, not just a symptom — triggering job loss and depression measured two years later. Between 2004 and 2010, as housing costs surged nationally, suicides attributed to forced housing displacement doubled. Worth stating plainly: critics note eviction often correlates with hardships that predate it, rather than single-handedly causing them — the honest reading is that instability compounds existing vulnerability, rarely the sole cause.

The Stakes

Who Will Profit, Who Will Lose

This is the question every other section here is really asking.

Economists call it the "homevoter" effect (Fischel, 2001): when the people voting on development also own homes that development will make more valuable, their financial interest and their public duty point the same direction — whether or not any individual acts in bad faith. That's a structural fact of small-town governance, not an accusation against any one person.

Who profits

Landowners who sell to developers. Homeowners whose values rise nearby. Developers and their investors. Albemarle's tax base, if the revenue flows there.

Who loses

Renters, who see costs rise with nothing to sell. Long-term low-income homeowners, taxed on paper wealth they can't spend without leaving. Anyone priced out of a market that never asked what they could afford.

If your home value quadruples like Crozet's did, the only way a longtime resident actually captures that gain is to sell and leave. The only winning move available to the people this town belongs to is to stop being here.

The Plan

None of This Is Hypothetical

Every tool below is legally available to Scottsville today. Tap each section to open it.

Tools the town can use now
Architectural review board
Real authority to reject designs that clash with the town's historic character, not just rubber-stamp them.
Updated comprehensive plan
The town's own current, defensible plan is its strongest legal footing against county-level decisions.
Adequate Public Facilities Ordinance
Ties new approvals to whether roads, schools, water and sewer can actually handle them.
Historic properties documentation
Formal listing makes demolition and insensitive redevelopment slower and harder.
Traffic impact studies
Requires developers to prove the roads can handle it before approval.
Strategic downsizing
Right-sizing town infrastructure deliberately, rather than assuming growth is the only path.
Impact fees
Charges developers directly for the real cost their project adds.
Proffer system
Virginia's own tool — developers offer cash or improvements for rezoning approval.
Sewage capacity studies
Establishes a hard, technical ceiling on how much growth infrastructure can support.
Zoning updates
Rewrites the town's code to reflect what residents actually want protected.
Property tax relief
Protects longtime low-income homeowners from being forced to sell to afford staying.
Right of first refusal
Gives tenants, owners, or the town first opportunity to buy before outside investors.
Rental registration & standards
Brings absentee landlords under enforceable town oversight.
Community land trust
Permanently affordable housing, insulated from market appreciation.
Transfer of development rights
Lets a landowner sell development rights to a receiving site elsewhere, preserving the original land.

Piedmont Environmental Council — regional advocacy against sprawl in this exact part of Virginia.
Land Trust of Virginia — conservation easements and land preservation statewide.
Agrarian Trust — protects farmland access through agricultural land trusts.
National Young Farmers Coalition — farmland affordability and access advocacy.
Housing Assistance Council — rural-specific affordable housing expertise.
Appalachian Voices — regional environmental and economic justice advocacy.
Appalachian Community Fund — grassroots funding for community organizing.
Land Loss Prevention Project — defends Black land ownership against loss and exploitation.
Institute for Justice — public interest law firm litigating property rights cases.
Preservation Virginia — statewide historic preservation advocacy and legal tools.
Institute for Local Self-Reliance — policy research on local economic control.
New Economy Coalition — network supporting community-controlled economic alternatives.
Partnership for Southern Equity — racial equity-focused regional policy advocacy.
Legal and procedural leverage
Maximize review timelines
Use every legally available day, rather than rushing approvals through.
Strict stormwater & erosion control
Enforceable standards, especially relevant on this flood-prone land.
Archaeological review requirements
Due diligence given centuries of settlement and a documented slave market on this ground.
Appeal all variance requests
Contest every deviation from existing zoning, rather than letting exceptions pass by default. Best paired with a published "yes, if" standard, so it reads as principled review rather than blanket obstruction.

Growth is coming to Scottsville whether the town shapes it or not. The only real question left is who it's for.
