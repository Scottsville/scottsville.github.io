Taxes & Property

Why the system works the way it does — and what you can do about it.

Scottsville has no property tax. This sounds like good news until you understand what it means: the town has almost no control over the revenue generated by the land and homes within its boundaries — and no mechanism to protect long-term residents from being priced out of a place they built.

Why Scottsville Has No Property Tax

Property owners in Scottsville pay real estate taxes to Albemarle County — not to the town. This is not unusual for small Virginia towns, but it has significant consequences. When property values rise, the county's revenue increases. The town's revenue does not. Scottsville funds its operations almost entirely through locally generated sources — meals tax, utility taxes, business licenses, and state-shared revenues — with meals tax alone accounting for more than 22% of total town revenue.

This means the town's fiscal health rises and falls with how many people are eating out locally. It has no stable base tied to land value, no automatic share of the appreciation that development generates, and no direct financial stake in what happens to property within its own boundaries.

When Tenaska's plant generates tax revenue, it goes to Fluvanna County — where the plant sits — not to Scottsville, whose residents live next to it. When West Village's new homes are assessed, that revenue goes to Albemarle County. The town that absorbs the social pressure of rising values and development captures almost none of the financial benefit.

Why the Town Can't Simply Fix This

Virginia operates under the Dillon Rule — one of the strictest applications of it in the country. Under Dillon Rule, localities can only do what the state legislature in Richmond explicitly authorizes them to do. Scottsville cannot decide on its own to implement a property tax, create a progressive tax structure, or design a homestead protection program. It needs state enabling legislation first.

Virginia's Constitution also requires that taxation be uniform within each class of property. A $500,000 home and a $150,000 home must be taxed at the same rate — a progressive property tax structure that protects lower-value homes is almost certainly unconstitutional under the current framework without a constitutional amendment.

What this means in practice is that the tools Scottsville would need to protect its existing residents from displacement pressure mostly don't exist at the town level, and the ones that do exist sit with Albemarle County — an entity whose financial interests are not perfectly aligned with Scottsville's. The county benefits from rising assessments and new development. Scottsville bears much of the social cost.

What Does Exist — and Who Qualifies

Albemarle County offers a real estate tax relief program for elderly and disabled homeowners that is more generous than most residents know. If you or someone you know might qualify, it is worth checking before the next tax bill arrives. The county expanded the program in 2025.

The relief tiers break down as follows based on combined income:

  • $48,825 or less — 100% relief. Your real estate tax bill is eliminated entirely.
  • $48,826 to $73,238 — 75% relief.
  • $73,239 to $85,444 — 50% relief.
  • Up to $100,650 — partial relief on a sliding scale.

There is also a separate full exemption for 100% service-connected disabled veterans and their surviving spouses. This exemption applies to up to 10 acres of property and requires documentation from the US Department of Veterans Affairs.

These programs are not automatic. You must apply.

Many eligible residents don't know these programs exist, or assume they don't qualify. The application window for new applicants runs January 1 through October 31. Existing recipients must recertify by April 1 each year or they lose their enrollment and must reapply from scratch.

If you think you or a neighbor might qualify, contact Albemarle County Finance directly — they offer in-person and virtual appointments with tax specialists who will walk you through the process at no cost.

If You Think Your Assessment Is Wrong

Albemarle County reassesses property annually at 100% of fair market value. Assessments went up 6.17% countywide in 2026. If your assessment doesn't reflect the actual condition or comparable sales in your area, you have the right to appeal — and the appeal process is free.

  • Contact the County Assessor's office first and ask about your assessment. This is the starting point before any formal process begins.
  • If unsatisfied, request an administrative review. Gather evidence — comparable recent sales, a recent independent appraisal, documentation of property condition issues.
  • If still unsatisfied, file an appeal with the Board of Equalization. The 2026 deadline was March 30, 2026. Watch for the annual notice for future deadlines.
  • As a last resort, appeals can be filed with the Virginia Tax Commissioner within 90 days of a final local determination.

The median real estate tax bill in Esmont — the lowest in Albemarle County — is $1,974 per year. In Keswick, the highest, it reaches $7,264. Scottsville's bills fall somewhere in this range depending on assessed value. If your bill feels out of proportion to what your home would actually sell for, appealing is worth pursuing.

What Would Actually Help — and Why It Probably Won't Happen Soon

The structural reforms that would genuinely protect Scottsville's existing residents require action at the state level, not the town level. They include:

A land value tax — taxing land at a higher rate than improvements — would discourage the LLC strategy of buying distressed properties and holding them vacant indefinitely. If carrying costs increase, owners are incentivized to do something with their land. Virginia has discussed this in policy circles but it has not been widely enacted.

A homestead exemption for long-term residents — capping assessment increases for people who have lived in their home for a defined period — would protect the longtime owner on a fixed income from being taxed out of a place they've lived for decades. Some Virginia localities have versions of this under existing state authorization.

Stronger proffer requirements in development approvals would ensure that new construction contributes directly to the community infrastructure it stresses. Virginia's proffer system requires offers to be voluntary, but a council with legal support can signal expectations clearly. West Village's 4-3 approval suggests those conversations were limited.

None of these require Scottsville to reinvent the wheel. They require Albemarle County and the Virginia General Assembly to extend tools that already exist in other jurisdictions to a town that currently has almost none of them.

What You Can Do Now

Check your eligibility for tax relief

If you are 65 or older, disabled, or a 100% service-connected disabled veteran, you may qualify for significant or complete elimination of your real estate tax bill. Apply through Albemarle County Finance. New applicants have until October 31.

Tell your neighbors

The people most likely to qualify for tax relief are the least likely to know it exists. An older neighbor living alone on Social Security in a house their family has owned for forty years may be paying a full tax bill they don't have to pay. Knowing about this program and passing it on is a genuine act of community.

Show up to Albemarle County Board of Supervisors meetings

Property tax rates, assessment policies, and relief program eligibility are all set at the county level. The Board of Supervisors holds an annual public hearing on tax rate changes. Scottsville residents have standing to participate in that process. The meetings are public. The agenda is posted in advance. Showing up matters.

Appeal your assessment if it feels wrong

The process is free. The burden of proof requires comparable evidence, but the Assessor's office will tell you what they based your assessment on and whether there's a reasonable case to make. Many homeowners never appeal because they assume it won't work. Some of them are right. Some of them are leaving money on the table.
