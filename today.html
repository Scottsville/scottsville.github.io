The Town Today

Population 600-something. No sidewalks. Iffy internet. Better than most places you've lived.

Scottsville is not special. It's one of a thousand towns this size scattered across rural America. What it is, is better — and that's a different thing.

The internet is unreliable. There are no sidewalks. Sometimes you get stuck behind a tractor on the way to pick your kids up from school. These are not complaints. They are the texture of the place, and they come with something you can't get in a city: people who actually see each other.

How This Town Works

This town judges people. Don't come in here thinking otherwise. It sees what you drive and when you leave and whether you waved back. Small places have long eyes and longer memories, and Scottsville is no different in that regard.

But what it remembers isn't what you look like or where you came from or how your name sounds in somebody else's mouth. It remembers whether you showed up. Whether you learned the names. Whether you were here, in some way that mattered, or whether you were just present, the way furniture is present.

Nobody here would step over a person lying on the sidewalk. Nobody. That's not a small thing. That's the whole thing, really, when you get down to it.

On Being a Newcomer

The word "newcomer" around here has almost nothing to do with time. I've watched people arrive from places most of us couldn't locate on a map, still feeling their way through the language, and inside of a year they weren't newcomers anymore. Not because a year had passed — something else had passed. Some threshold of intention. Attention. Effort, maybe, though that word is too transactional.

And I've known people who lived here a long time and stayed new the whole while, which isn't a judgment exactly, just an observation. Some people live inside themselves regardless of their zip code. This place will let you do that if you want. It'll also let you in, if you're trying to get in.
