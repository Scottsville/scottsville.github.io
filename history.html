Chapter I

The Monacan People

10,000 BCE — 1607 CE and beyond

Long before there was a Scottsville, long before there was a Virginia, long before there was anyone to name this particular bend in the river, there were the Monacan. Their presence here reaches back at least ten thousand years — a span of time so vast it makes the entire history of European settlement in the Americas look like an afternoon.

The Monacan Nation belonged to the Eastern Siouan language family, related to the Tutelo, the Occaneechi, and the Saponi peoples. Their territory once covered roughly half of what is now Virginia — most of the Piedmont region, portions of the Blue Ridge, and stretches of the Shenandoah Valley. They lived above the falls of the James River, in the interior highlands that the coastal Algonquian-speaking confederacy of Tsenacomoco never fully controlled. The Monacan and the Powhatan peoples were traditional rivals, a rivalry that predated European contact by centuries.

The horseshoe bend of the James River at Scottsville was not incidental to Monacan life. The river was everything — road, larder, boundary, and sacred presence. They worshipped it as a bringer of life, which it was. The fertile bottomlands of the bend produced food. The river itself provided fish. The surrounding piedmont provided game, timber, and clay. The Monacan village site of Monasukapanough, documented by archaeologists and occupied from approximately 1300 to 1670 CE, sat within what is now the Scottsville area, its burial mounds once rising twelve feet above the landscape.

They worshipped the river as a bringer of life. They had been doing so for ten thousand years before anyone else arrived to name it.

When the English landed at Jamestown in 1607, the Monacan remained in their Piedmont homeland, somewhat insulated from initial contact by distance and geography. But the pressures of colonization moved inland. Disease preceded settlers, moving faster than any army. Trade networks entangled communities. By the late 17th century, Monacan populations had been dramatically reduced and their territorial sovereignty steadily eroded.

The early 1800s brought a particularly sorrowful chapter. Monacan people continued to travel to their ancestral burial mounds along the James to worship — journeys that required crossing land that had been claimed, fenced, and deeded to others. Those journeys stopped when the mounds were plowed flat. The physical memory of their presence was erased from the landscape by the same agricultural economy that had displaced them from it.

The Monacan Nation survived. Today they are a federally recognized tribe of approximately 2,300 citizens, headquartered at Bear Mountain in Amherst County — still in their ancestral homeland, still on the James River watershed. The Scottsville Museum holds a collection of Monacan artifacts recovered from the area. The town formally acknowledges the Monacan Nation as the traditional custodians of the land where Scottsville now stands.

They were here first. They are here still.

Chapter II

Scott's Landing

1720s — 1818

In the 1720s, the first English settlers began arriving at the horseshoe bend of the James, drawn by the same things that had drawn the Monacan for millennia — fertile bottomland, river access, the natural harbor formed by the bend itself. Among the earliest was a family named Scott, and the landing on the northern bank of the river began to carry their name.

The settlement grew around fifteen acres owned by Dr. John Scott. Edward Scott's house served as an informal courthouse before a proper one was built. The location, at what was then the western edge of organized Virginia settlement, made it a natural administrative center for the vast and largely unmapped interior.

1744 Men gather at Scott's Landing to establish the first government of Albemarle County. Among them is Peter Jefferson — Thomas Jefferson's father — who serves as justice for the newly formed county. Scott's Landing becomes Albemarle's first county seat, predating Charlottesville by nearly two decades.

Peter Jefferson's presence at that founding gathering is a detail worth pausing on. His son Thomas, who would later practice law in the Scottsville courthouse and visit the area frequently throughout his life, was not yet born. The town that would become Scottsville existed before Monticello, before the University of Virginia, before Charlottesville itself. When Thomas Jefferson later sought refuge from British forces during the Revolutionary War, he passed through Scottsville — the historical record notes he took the opportunity to examine a cave in the river bluffs.

Randolph Jefferson, Thomas's younger brother, settled in the area and his descendants shaped the town's early commercial identity. Peter Field Jefferson, Randolph's son and Thomas's nephew, became one of Scottsville's most prominent early businessmen — his rise and fall tracking the town's own arc across seven decades of frontier, canal boom, and Civil War.

1762 The county seat is relocated to Charlottesville. Scottsville loses its governmental primacy but retains its commercial importance as a river trading post.
1818 The Virginia General Assembly formally establishes Scottsville as a town. It is officially incorporated in 1833.

By its incorporation, Scottsville had already established its identity as a river town — a tobacco inspection station with a ferry crossing, a gathering place for commerce moving between the interior and the coast. What came next would make it genuinely significant.

Chapter III

The Canal Era

1840 — 1865

Everything changed in 1840, when the James River and Kanawha Canal was completed to Scottsville. The canal transformed what had been a promising river landing into one of the most commercially significant interior ports in Virginia. For twenty-five years — the 1840s and 1850s, called its golden era — Scottsville was the largest inland port above Richmond on the James River and the largest grain market in Virginia.

The agricultural wealth of the entire Shenandoah Valley moved through this town. Tobacco, grain, flour, and iron came east on the canal toward Richmond and the sea. Coffee, tea, molasses, sugar, and manufactured goods came west, bound for the interior. At its height, the canal carried tonnage that would seem implausible for a town of Scottsville's size today.

The 1840s and 1850s were Scottsville's golden era. It was the largest inland port on the James River above Richmond — a town that mattered far beyond its size.

The vessels that made this possible were the batteaux — long, flat-bottomed river craft designed specifically for the James, capable of carrying as many as eleven hogsheads of tobacco (roughly 11,000 pounds) through the river's curves and the canal's locks. The batteaumen who poled these craft were among the most skilled rivermen in colonial and early American Virginia. They were largely enslaved men and freedmen, absent from their home plantations for weeks at a time, navigating hundreds of miles of river and canal with extraordinary competence. Their names are mostly lost to the historical record. Their labor is not — it is embedded in the very commerce that built the town.

The canal also brought packet boats — the luxury liners of the James River system — carrying passengers in relative comfort between Lynchburg and Richmond, with Scottsville as a principal stop. For a brief and prosperous quarter-century, the horseshoe bend of the James was a place where the world passed through.

1840 The James River and Kanawha Canal is completed to Scottsville. The town becomes the largest inland port above Richmond — the chief shipping hub for the Shenandoah Valley's agricultural output.

Then, in the span of a single afternoon in March 1865, it ended.

Chapter IV

When War Came

1861 — 1865

Scottsville did not escape the Civil War. During the conflict, buildings in the town served as Confederate hospitals. The war moved through the surrounding county in multiple directions, and by early 1865 the Confederacy was in its final weeks. Then came Sheridan.

March 6, 1865 At 3 p.m., the first of Union General Philip Sheridan's 10,000 cavalrymen — under Generals Wesley Merritt, Thomas Devin, and George Armstrong Custer — enter Scottsville unopposed. Sheridan chooses Cliffside on Warren Street as his headquarters. General Merritt takes Old Hall on Harrison Street.

Sheridan's orders were not to occupy Scottsville but to destroy it as an economic and logistical asset. His cavalry burned a woolen factory, a candle factory, a large five-story flouring mill, machine shops, and tobacco warehouses. Three canal boats were captured — one loaded with Confederate artillery shells, two with commissary stores and tobacco. Private dwellings near the mill were scorched by the intense heat of the burning structures.

Then they destroyed the canal locks.

Upriver from Scottsville to New Market, the lock system of the James River and Kanawha Canal was systematically disabled. Seven bridges at Columbia were destroyed. This was not incidental damage — it was the deliberate erasure of infrastructure that had taken decades and enormous capital to build. The canal that had made Scottsville the largest inland port above Richmond was rendered inoperable. The commerce that had defined the town's golden era would never return.

You destroy the locks, you destroy the town's reason for being what it was. What happened after — the slower pace, the smaller scale, the quiet — had a specific cause and a specific date.

There is a human detail from that March afternoon worth preserving. Major James Christian Hill, a Scottsville businessman and commander of the 46th Virginia Regiment, had been seriously wounded at Petersburg the previous July. He was convalescing at Chester, the home of his mother-in-law on James River Road. Knowing he would likely be arrested and taken away, Hill wrote a letter to General Sheridan explaining the family's situation — the invalid officer, the women of the household, the servants — and asking for a guard to protect the house. Sheridan granted it.

Five weeks after leaving Scottsville, Sheridan's cavalry was at Appomattox. The war ended. The canal did not recover. Scottsville's manufacturing facilities, the Cvillepedia record notes, never fully recovered either — while the Charlottesville woolen mill that Sheridan's men also burned was rebuilt by the Marchant family within years.

The difference between a town that recovers from destruction and one that doesn't is rarely about the destruction itself. It is about what the town had that made recovery worth investing in. Without the canal, Scottsville had less reason to attract that investment. The locks were gone. The traffic had rerouted. The golden era was over.

Chapter V

The River Remembers

1870 — present

Scottsville sits on the outside of a horseshoe bend in the James River. This geography gave it its commercial identity — the natural harbor, the deep water, the turning basin for canal boats. It also made the town extraordinarily vulnerable to flooding. The river that built Scottsville has spent a century and a half trying to reclaim it.

Since 1870, the James at Scottsville has reached flood stage — twenty feet above mean low water — on 44 recorded occasions. Fourteen of those have been classified as major floods, with crests above twenty-four feet. The worst came in June 1972, when Hurricane Agnes pushed the river to 34.02 feet, carrying 301,000 cubic feet of water per second past the town. The flood of September 1870, the first in the recorded record, crested at 30.7 feet. Hurricane Camille's remnants brought 30 feet in August 1969. Hurricane Juan in November 1985 brought 31.77 feet.

Each time, the town absorbed the water and the mud and the damage, and began again. The continuity of that response — not recovery, exactly, but resumption — is one of the defining facts of Scottsville's character. The people who stayed through the floods did not do so because they had nowhere to go. They stayed because the place was theirs.

Chapter VI

Industry & the Long Decline

1865 — 2009

In the years after the Civil War, Scottsville contracted. The canal was gone. The Virginia Central Railroad had reached Charlottesville by 1850 — while Scottsville was still a canal town — establishing Charlottesville as the region's railroad hub before Scottsville could compete. When the Richmond and Allegheny Railroad finally arrived in Scottsville in the 1880s, it came built on the ruins of what it replaced: the old James River and Kanawha Canal towpath, repurposed as a rail bed. The tracks that run through Scottsville today — now operated by CSX — follow the same route the canal boats once traveled. But by the time the railroad arrived, the commercial momentum had long since shifted north to Charlottesville. The railroad confirmed the new order rather than restoring the old one. The major flood of 1870, just five years after Sheridan's cavalry, dealt another blow to a town already struggling to find its post-canal identity.

The late 19th and early 20th centuries brought recurring floods and modest recovery between them. The town persisted — its historic buildings accumulating, its families staying, its character solidifying into something quieter and more insular than the bustling inland port of the 1840s. It was no longer a place the world passed through. It was a place people lived.

1944 The United States Rubber Company builds a plant on 51 acres just west of Scottsville, producing rayon tire cord for military vehicles during World War II. The plant is the largest economic development in the town's post-canal history. At the end of the war in 1945, Uniroyal purchases the plant and continues manufacturing operations.

The plant changed everything for a time. It brought stable employment, a payroll that circulated through the local economy, and a reason for families to stay rather than leave for the cities. For sixty-five years, the tire plant was the economic anchor of Scottsville — its largest employer, the institution around which the town's working life organized itself.

The plant passed through multiple ownership changes over the decades. Uniroyal. Uniroyal Goodrich. Through the 1990s, as NAFTA accelerated the offshoring of American manufacturing and the tire industry consolidated, employment at the plant contracted. The Korean conglomerate Hyosung acquired the facility and operated it into the 2000s. But the forces of global manufacturing economics were larger than any single facility could resist.

2009 Hyosung closes the Scottsville tire plant. The town's largest employer for sixty-five years goes dark. The 51-acre site — the former US Rubber plant that once employed hundreds — sits vacant. It remains empty to this day.

The closure in 2009 was not simply the loss of jobs. It was the removal of the economic rationale that had sustained the town through the second half of the twentieth century. Population, which had recovered somewhat during the plant's operating years, declined. The population that had reached 566 in 2010 — partly buoyed by the plant's legacy — fell to 522 by 2020.

The parallel with 1865 is not accidental. In both cases, an outside actor made a decision — to destroy the canal locks, to close the manufacturing facility — that removed the economic engine the town depended on. In neither case did Scottsville have meaningful control over that decision. In both cases, what followed was a long, slow contraction that the community survived through the same mechanisms it always had: staying, knowing each other, holding on.

Chapter VII

The Modern Era

1989 — present

The 1989 flood control project was the first genuinely good news in Scottsville's infrastructure story in over a century. It did not make the town prosperous, but it removed a recurring catastrophe from the calendar. For the first time, the river was a neighbor rather than a periodic destroyer. The James, which had defined Scottsville from its first settlement, was finally managed rather than merely endured.

The town's historic district — over 150 buildings representing commercial, residential, religious, and industrial architecture from multiple centuries — became a resource in the post-industrial era. Tourism, river recreation, and the proximity to Charlottesville gave Scottsville a different kind of relevance. The Canal Basin Square opened as an outdoor heritage park. The Scottsville Museum became a repository for the town's extraordinary layered history. The James River Batteau Festival, revived in 1985 and running annually, reconnects the river's present to its past.

May 1, 2004 The Tenaska Virginia Generating Station, a 940-megawatt natural gas power plant, comes online in Fluvanna County adjacent to Scottsville. The project promises economic benefit to the region. It employs 29 permanent workers, approximately one-third of whom live outside the county. Its tax revenue accrues to Fluvanna County.

The 21st century has brought Scottsville the pressures of its own relative virtue. A town of 600 people that retained its historic fabric, its community cohesion, and its position on a beautiful river — twenty minutes from Charlottesville — became, in the real estate vocabulary of the era, a discovery. The population that had fallen to as low as 239 in 2000 began, slowly, to recover. New residents arrived, drawn by exactly the qualities that generations of hardship had preserved.

With them came the dynamics that have reshaped small towns across rural America. New development — the West Village project, a 36-unit residential development approved by a 4-3 council vote — broke ground on the Bird Street corridor. A second Tenaska plant, the Expedition Generating Station at 1,500 megawatts, received approval from the Fluvanna County Board of Supervisors in 2025 over the Planning Commission's recommendation of denial and community objections that the first plant's promised benefits had not materialized.

Present Scottsville's population stands at approximately 543. Its median household income is $46,563 — roughly half of Albemarle County's $104,392 median. Its poverty rate of 14.8% is more than double the county's 7.09%. The vacant tire plant site remains undeveloped after fifteen years. The historic district retains over 150 buildings from multiple centuries of habitation.

What Scottsville carries into its future is what it has always carried: a community that survived the destruction of its canal, the flooding of its streets, the closure of its factory, and the slow drift of economics away from places like it. The people who stayed through all of that built something that does not appear on any balance sheet. They built the knowledge of who to call when something breaks. They built the slow trust of people who have watched each other struggle and helped each other up.

The Monacan worshipped the river as a bringer of life. The batteaumen poled their heavy loads through the horseshoe bend in weather that would have stopped lesser people. The families that stayed through twenty-one floods did not do so because the math worked out. They did so because the place was theirs, and leaving felt like a kind of death that flooding never did.

That continuity is Scottsville's most valuable possession. It is more valuable than the historic district, more valuable than the river view, more valuable than the proximity to Charlottesville. It cannot be purchased. It cannot be replaced. And it is the one thing no outside actor — no cavalry, no flood, no developer with a prospectus — has yet managed to take away.

The town didn't leave. That is, in the end, the whole of the story.
